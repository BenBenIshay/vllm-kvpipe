\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The applicability window is a result}

The gate of Eq.~\ref{eq:gate} and the multiplier of Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} together
form a deployment rule that can be evaluated before building anything: a second
KV tier pays when the latency target exceeds the full-pool step time, and it
pays in proportion to $\HBM/\Ckv$ --- how much of device memory the weights
consumed. Both exclude configurations the field routinely evaluates on. An 8B
model on a 96\,GB device leaves so much memory after weights that there is
little to buy once the fixed cost is charged, and a latency target below the
device's own step time makes the
scheduler latency-bound rather than capacity-bound, where offloading is
strictly harmful.

We state the window as a finding rather than a caveat because the alternative
is worse: a technique whose applicability is unstated gets evaluated where it
happens to win.

\subsection{Generality across memory tiers and attention backends}
\label{sec:discussion:general}

Nothing in the design is specific to HBM-plus-DRAM or to one attention kernel.
The system needs three things from a substrate: a memory larger and slower than
the device's, a path to it whose bandwidth can be measured, and an attention
implementation that can read a block table. A third tier --- CXL-attached
memory, or NVMe --- adds a second budget and a second affinity threshold and
leaves the structure unchanged. What does not survive is the hiding argument: a
tier whose transfer cannot fit inside a layer's compute window is a capacity
tier for idle requests, not a bandwidth tier for active ones, and
Eq.~\ref{eq:gate} decides which it is. Similarly, a zero-copy kernel can replace
our staging buffer through the same backend interface without touching the
policy; the two are ordered by operating point rather than by quality
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:coherence}).

Moving to a new platform requires re-measuring five quantities: achieved
interconnect bandwidth, achieved device bandwidth for weights and for paged KV,
the overlap efficiency $\effo$, and the fixed per-step cost. The last two are
the ones the second gate turns on, and $\effo$ is the one with no sensitivity
analysis anywhere: at $\effo = 1$ the four rows of Table~\ref{tab:gain} read
$\times$0.81, $\times$0.85, $\times$1.07 and $\times$1.22 on throughput --- so
perfect overlap flips the sign for the two favourable rows and not for the
others. The rules do not change; their inputs do. This is
why we report the fixed cost separately from the per-byte cost rather than as a
single slope --- only one of the two shrinks when the interconnect improves, and
a system calibrated on their sum will mispredict itself on the next generation.

\subsection{Anti-scaling}

The gain shrinks as device memory grows, and for our primary model it does not
merely shrink: Qwen2.5-32B on a 144\,GB device (141 usable) leaves $\Ckv \approx 71$\,GB, at
which point the workload is no longer capacity-bound and the gate stops
admitting entirely. Both factors of Eq.~\ref{eq:gain} trend the same way across
a hardware generation, since HBM bandwidth and capacity have grown faster than
host-to-device bandwidth. This is the same structural decay we identify in
compute-offload systems (\S\ref{sec:related}), and it would be dishonest to cite
that against them without stating it about ourselves.

What bears the other way is model growth: 405B-class models push $\HBM/\Ckv$ up,
not down, so the configurations where the technique pays are becoming more common
even as each device generation makes them rarer. We do \emph{not} claim
mixture-of-experts models as part of that trend, although their weight footprints
are large: Eq.~\ref{eq:step} charges every weight once per step, which is false
for MoE, so our law does not apply to them without a rederivation we have not
done. And the gate is itself the deployment rule --- a system that switches
itself off where it cannot help is behaving correctly.

\subsection{Composition with orthogonal techniques}

Methods that \emph{shrink} the KV cache compose with a system that
\emph{relocates} it. Quantizing KV scales both cohorts together, leaving their
ratio --- and so the value of a second tier --- unchanged. Quantizing
\emph{weights} does the opposite: it returns memory to $\Ckv$ and removes the
amplifier, by the same mechanism through which a larger device reduces the gain.
Sparse and low-rank methods trade exactness for size, where a tier moves bytes
without altering them, so the two address different axes and could be combined.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Scope of the evaluation.} Every measurement is on a single A100-80
over PCIe --- the regime our own model says cannot pay. The positive claims rest
on a coherent interconnect we have not yet measured, and \S\ref{sec:eval} marks
each as a prediction. We evaluate one device: tensor parallelism would introduce
collectives into precisely the metric under measurement, and adding a second GPU
is also the most obvious \emph{alternative} to a second memory tier, which we
therefore cannot price against it.

\paragraph{Scope of the model.} Eq.~\ref{eq:step} charges every weight once,
which is false for mixture-of-experts models, so the law as written does not
apply to them. It assumes dense GQA attention: an MLA-class model compresses KV
enough that the regime changes character (\S\ref{sec:related}). It assumes
uniform context, and the cancellation of \S\ref{sec:model:cancel} is exact only
in that limit. And it charges nothing for host DRAM bandwidth, which on a
480\,GB Grace configuration is of the same order as the link itself and would
bound $\Xlink$ from behind.

\paragraph{The regime is real but not universal.} The configurations that clear
both gates are those where weights consume most of the device --- 64\,GB of
weights on a 96\,GB part. An operator facing exactly that can also quantize the
weights or add a GPU, and both are common. We think the regime is nonetheless
worth serving: quantization is an accuracy decision that a serving team often
cannot take unilaterally, and adding a GPU doubles the cost of the node to
recover memory that is already installed and idle beside it. But a reader who
believes those alternatives are always available should read this paper as
bounding when the technique could help, not as arguing it is the best response.

\paragraph{Mechanism and engineering.} The prefill transport is emulated: KV
reaches the host pool through a local handover whose cost we measure
(\S\ref{sec:impl:stash}), not through a production prefill node's network path.
Speculative decoding is unsupported on the host path, which assumes one query
token per step. The pinned host mirrors are real memory, sized to the configured
maximum concurrency and context. The fixed per-step cost is a floor of the
current design rather than a tuned result --- cross-layer batching of the
transfer issue would reduce it and we have not built it --- and since it is the
quantity the second gate turns on (\S\ref{sec:model:omits}), our own headline
result is bounded by an engineering number rather than by a physical one.

\paragraph{Generality of the interconnect claim.} We measure PCIe and model
NVLink-C2C. Whether the argument carries to CXL-attached memory or to other
vendors' coherent links depends on the same three quantities
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:general}), but we have measured none of them there.

\subsection{Staging versus zero-copy}
\label{sec:discussion:coherence}

Our host tier stages KV through a device rolling slot; DirectKV's kernel reads
CPU-resident KV directly. We built on a staged backend because it runs on PCIe
as well as on a coherent link --- which is what let us measure the negative
regime at all --- and because their kernel is SM90a-only. Our results are
therefore \emph{conservative with respect to mechanism}.

The two are ordered by operating point rather than by quality, and the ordering
matters for where each belongs. Staging overlaps a layer's fetch with the whole
of the previous layer's compute; a zero-copy kernel overlaps it only with the
attention tile it is already computing, which at decode's arithmetic intensity
is almost no compute at all. Staging buys a layer-sized hiding window and pays
two extra HBM passes for it, so it wins while the per-step transfer fits inside
a layer's compute and loses once the transfer exceeds it. That is why DirectKV,
which places 100\% of KV on the host and therefore can never fit a compute
window, is right to use zero-copy, and why we --- sizing a selected subset to fit
by construction --- are right to stage. Appendix~\ref{app:coherence} works the
crossover through and notes the one cost a coherent link would remove
regardless: the device-to-host write of each step's new $K,V$.

\subsection{Placement by hardware, policy by scheduler}

A further consequence, which we flag as future work because we cannot test it.
With a single coherent copy, demoting a request to the host tier becomes a
policy decision rather than a data movement, and promotion could be lazy and
partial --- the hardware's page migration relocating hot blocks into device
memory while the memory system supplies hints rather than issuing transfers.
That is an appealing division of labour, and to our knowledge nobody has
evaluated it for KV cache.

We are wary of it for one specific reason. Hardware page migration operates at
4\,KB or 2\,MB granularity and migrates on access, which is structurally the
same reactive, fine-grained policy whose migration overhead dominates in the
token-granularity placement literature~\cite{calkv}. Our own claim is that
request-granularity movement avoids that thrash because whole requests move
once, at controlled moments. Handing placement back to a fault-driven mechanism
gives that property up, and whether the hardware's migration is cheap enough to
survive it is an empirical question we have not answered.
