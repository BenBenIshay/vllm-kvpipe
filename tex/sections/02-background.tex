\section{Background and system model}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Capacity, not compute, caps the batch}
\label{sec:background:capacity}

A decode step is memory-bound by a wide margin: it reads every weight and every
resident KV byte once, at an arithmetic intensity equal to the GQA group size
(4--8 on current models) against an \emph{achieved} balance point of order 100
FLOP/byte (Appendix~\ref{app:params}). This is the premise the offloading
literature is built on --- FlexGen~\cite{flexgen}, Pie~\cite{pie} and
DirectKV~\cite{directkv} all exploit it --- and it is not unanimous:
NanoFlow~\cite{nanoflow} argues serving becomes \emph{compute}-bound once
operations of different resource classes are overlapped within a device. The two
claims are about different batch sizes, and \S\ref{sec:related} gives the
crossing point we measure.

\textbf{Which of the two reads dominates is a property of the model, not of the
operating point}, and the spread is large: at a full pool on an A100-80,
Llama-3.1-8B spends $11.1$ of $78$\,ms on weights (14\% weights) where
Qwen2.5-32B spends $45.2$ of $53$ (85\% weights). Both are capacity-bound, on
identical hardware, at opposite ends of one axis. The weight terms follow from the
measured $1935$\,GB/s peak; the step times they divide are projected
(\S\ref{sec:model:modeldep}).

That spread is what makes a second tier worth having, and its direction is the
opposite of the intuitive one. The weight read is a per-step window in which the
link carries nothing; the larger the weights relative to the device, the longer
that window \emph{and} the less HBM is left for KV. So configurations where
weights dominate have both the most idle link time and the least device memory:
Qwen2.5-32B is favourable \emph{because} it is 85\% weights, Llama-3.1-8B
unfavourable because it is 14\%. \S\ref{sec:model} makes this quantitative and
\S\ref{sec:model:omits} gives the cost that can cancel it.

\subsection{Host memory as a tier for actively decoding requests}

Offloading KV to host memory is an old idea that a new interconnect has changed.
Over PCIe~4 at roughly 25\,GB/s, streaming a request's KV every decode step costs
more than the step itself, so host memory could only be a \emph{swap} target:
somewhere to park a request that is not running. At NVLink-C2C's measured
419\,GB/s it becomes a tier an \emph{actively decoding} request can occupy.
Several systems already decode from host-resident KV (\S\ref{sec:related}); what
none does is \emph{choose, per request,} which tier such a request lives in ---
continuous batching, prefix reuse, eviction and hierarchical caching all decide
residency for requests that are \emph{not} currently running.

\paragraph{Host DRAM is the first such tier, not the only one.} The direction of
travel is towards accelerators attached to more than one kind of memory:
CXL-attached DRAM behind a coherent link, and high-bandwidth flash placed between
DRAM and storage, are both being built for exactly the capacity problem above.
Nothing in this design is specific to host DRAM. What it asks of a tier is three
things, and they are the right questions to ask of any of them: that the tier be
\textbf{larger} than device memory, that the path to it have bandwidth we can
\textbf{measure} at runtime, and that a request's per-step traffic over that path
\textbf{fit inside the compute of one layer} --- the last deciding whether the
tier can hold \emph{decoding} requests or only parked ones. The capacity model of
\S\ref{sec:model} is written in those three quantities and nothing else, so it
transfers to a new tier by re-profiling rather than by re-deriving
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:general}).

It also changes what the movement should be. \emph{Swapping} relocates ownership:
a unit lives on one side, is brought over, and must be written back if it changed,
so every offloaded byte crosses the link twice per generated token.
\emph{Streaming} keeps the host copy authoritative and treats the device copy as a
cache, so the return leg carries only the token just produced. That is a factor of
two on the resource which bounds the whole technique, and it follows from what the
offloaded unit \emph{is} rather than from any optimisation.

\subsection{System model: a disaggregated decode node}
\label{sec:background:pd}

We target a \textbf{decode node in a prefill/decode-disaggregated
deployment}~\cite{distserve,splitwise,mooncake}: prefill runs on separate
hardware, and a request arrives with its KV already computed. The deployment is
widely used and supported by the major serving stacks, because prefill and decode
have opposite resource profiles. The assumption does real work --- on a decode
node a host-tier request's KV can be delivered \emph{into host memory} and never
occupy an HBM block, not even transiently, so such requests are \textbf{additive}:
served in addition to a full resident cohort rather than displacing part of it.

In a combined prefill+decode engine this is false, and not subtly: a request must
be prefilled somewhere, so its KV lands in HBM first and is reclaimed only
afterwards, and both cohorts then draw on the same headroom. We measured the
result --- the served total creeps toward the all-HBM capacity from below and
never passes it, at any setting of the resident cap, because a host-tier request
whose prefill stays resident frees nothing while still paying interconnect every
step (\S\ref{sec:eval:capacity}). Colocated chunked-prefill serving remains
common, so that is a real limit rather than a technicality. The cost of the
assumption is that KV must reach host DRAM; \S\ref{sec:impl:stash} implements that
handover and measures it, so the reader can price the assumption rather than
accept it.
