\section{Fusing the Attention Calls}\label{sec:fusion-app}\label{sec:fused}

\input{figs/fig-fused}

\planned{This design is priced, not implemented. Everything measured in the
paper uses the two-call path.}

\paragraph{Today.} Attention runs as two calls per layer (\cref{fig:fused}a;
\S\ref{sec:addressing}): the first over every row against the paged pool, the
second over the rider rows against the slot, with a gather and a scatter.

\paragraph{Planned.} A single call in which each row carries its own KV region,
the paged pool or the current slot, selected inside the kernel, with no gather
or scatter (\cref{fig:fused}b). Because the rider table is already built once
per step (\S\ref{sec:addressing}), fusion needs $k$ tables per step, not a
per-layer rebuild. The second call is the larger part of the fixed cost $F$
that remains; the ablation below measures it.

\input{figs/fig-abl-fusion}

\paragraph{What fusing the attention calls could buy.} \Cref{fig:abl-fusion}
removes the second attention call's kernel, then the whole call. The call costs
1.21\,ms of the 3.36\,ms fixed cost, almost all of it the kernel and its launches
(1.16\,ms), and 0.13\,ms per rider for gathering and scattering rider rows. A
fused kernel removes the gather and scatter outright; it removes at most the
$F$ share, since it still performs the attention arithmetic. Fusion's value is
therefore a range, not a number, and its upper end grows with context: with
one resident, the second call's kernel costs 1.3\,ms of $F$ at context 512
and 1.6\,ms at 14\,724. Carried to the target as in \cref{tab:ladder}, fusion would raise the gain
from $1.20\times$ to $1.21$--$1.25\times$.
