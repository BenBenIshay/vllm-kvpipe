\subsection{Zero-copy, coherence, and what is actually free}
\label{sec:discussion:coherence}

Our host tier stages: each layer's rider KV is copied from a pinned host mirror
into a rolling GPU buffer just before that layer's attention. On hardware whose
CPU and GPU share a cache-coherent address space, the obvious question is why
we copy at all --- if the GPU can read host memory coherently, could the mirror
and the device tensor not simply be the same memory, kept consistent by the
hardware at no cost? The question is worth answering carefully, because the
intuitive answer is wrong in an instructive way.

\paragraph{The cost splits in two, and only one half is intrinsic.} A rider
costs us a \emph{transport} term --- the bytes that must cross the link,
measured at 4.66\,ms per rider at 8K on PCIe --- and a \emph{staging} term: the
rolling-slot bookkeeping, the per-layer scatter, the device-to-host write of
each newly generated token, and the gather/scatter that splits the batch into
two attention calls. The staging term is most of our fixed per-step cost, and
it is an artifact of the mechanism rather than of the problem. A zero-copy
attention kernel that reads host addresses directly~\cite{directkv} eliminates
it by construction: there is no staging buffer to manage, and no mirror to keep
consistent because the host copy simply \emph{is} the KV cache. The
synchronisation cost is not something to optimise; with one copy it does not
exist. Our measured fixed cost should therefore be read as a tax for not having
that kernel, and \S\ref{sec:eval} reports what removing it is worth.

\paragraph{Coherence does not make the transport free.} What it makes it is
\emph{implicit}. Cache coherence is a guarantee about \emph{visibility}, not a
mechanism for cheaper \emph{transport}: when the GPU touches a line resident in
host memory, the hardware fetches it over the interconnect at that moment, at
link bandwidth. The bytes are the same bytes. What changes is who decides
\emph{when} they move --- and for us that is precisely the wrong thing to give
away. Our design overlaps a rider's layer-$L$ transfer with the compute of
earlier layers, which is where 78\% of it disappears. A demand-fetched coherent
read happens when the kernel touches the line: inside the attention kernel, at
cache-line granularity, with no prefetch distance and the latency fully
exposed. Replacing one large pipelined bulk transfer with many small
demand-fetched ones is the classic unified-memory paging cliff, and we would
expect it to be worse than staging, not free.

The productive reading of the idea is therefore not ``let coherence
synchronise two copies'' but ``keep one copy and let the kernel stream it
under software control'' --- which is what a zero-copy attention kernel already
does. Coherence is what makes that legal; it is not itself the optimisation.

\paragraph{Where coherence buys something a zero-copy read does not.} One place:
the \emph{write} path. Each decode step projects new $K,V$ for every rider, and
we currently copy those to the host mirror explicitly to keep it authoritative.
If a rider's KV blocks are mapped host memory, the cache-write during attention
lands there directly and coherence handles visibility --- no explicit
device-to-host copy, no dual write, and none of the bookkeeping that keeps two
copies in step. The effect is small in absolute terms (we measured an
asynchronous version of that copy at 1.6\,ms per step) but it removes a class
of state rather than an amount of time, and that state is where migration bugs
live.

\paragraph{A policy question this opens, which we leave open.} If a rider's KV
exists once, coherently, then \emph{tier migration becomes nearly free in one
direction}: demoting a request to the host tier is a policy decision rather
than a copy. Promotion could then be lazy and partial, with the hardware's own
page migration relocating hot blocks into device memory while the scheduler
supplies hints rather than issuing transfers. That is an appealing division of
labour --- placement by hardware at page granularity, policy by the scheduler
at request granularity --- and to our knowledge nobody has evaluated it for KV
cache.

We flag two reasons it is future work rather than a result. First, we cannot
test it: it requires a coherent platform and a kernel we do not own. Second,
and more interesting, it risks the very failure we argue against elsewhere in
this paper. Hardware page migration operates at 4\,KB or 2\,MB granularity and
migrates on access, which is structurally the same reactive, fine-grained
policy whose migration overhead dominates in the token-granularity placement
literature~\cite{calkv}. Our own claim is that \emph{request}-granularity
movement avoids that thrash because whole requests move once, at controlled
moments. Handing placement back to a page-fault-driven mechanism would give
that property up, and whether the hardware's migration is cheap enough to
survive it is an empirical question we have not answered. It is the right next
experiment; it is not a free win, and we would rather say so than imply the
coherent link makes the problem disappear.
