\section{When does host-KV offload pay?}
\label{sec:model}

Before describing a policy we establish what there is to gain, because the
answer is narrow and the boundaries are sharp. This section derives four
closed-form results, reports which of them survived contact with hardware, and
uses the survivors to explain a published result that its own authors leave
unexplained.

\subsection{Setup}

Let $\Xlink$ be the achieved interconnect bandwidth between host memory and the
GPU, $\BW$ the achieved HBM bandwidth, $\HBM$ the device memory, $W$ the bytes
of model weights, and $\Ckv = \HBM - W - \mathcal{R}$ the memory left for KV cache, where
$\mathcal{R}$ is a device reserve for activations, workspace and allocator
fragmentation ($4$\,GB on the parts we consider). Every equation and table below
uses this definition. Let $S$
be the context length and $\btok{}$ the KV bytes per token.

A decode step reads every weight once and every resident KV byte once, and the
two reads do not achieve the same bandwidth. Writing $\BW_w$ and $\BW_{kv}$ for
what each actually gets,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:step}
\mathrm{ITL}(B) \;=\; \frac{W}{\BW_w} \;+\; \frac{B \cdot S \cdot \btok{}}{\BW_{kv}}.
\end{equation}
%
That weights stream faster than paged KV is unsurprising; we dwell on it only
because the gap is large enough to change the answer. On A100-80 we measure
$\BW_w = 1450$\,GB/s while $\BW_{kv}$ falls from $1480$ to $890$\,GB/s as the KV
pool grows (Appendix~\ref{app:params}), and \S\ref{sec:model:modeldep} gives the
consequence.

The capacity-limited batch is $B_{\mathrm{cap}} = \Ckv / (S \btok{})$: the point at
which KV, not arithmetic, exhausts the device. A rider's KV lives in host memory
and streams over the interconnect during the step. A step of length
$\mathrm{ITL}$ cannot carry more than $\mathrm{ITL}\cdot\Xlink$ bytes across the
link whatever else is true, so the rider cohort is bounded by
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:boff}
B_{\mathrm{off}} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{ITL} \cdot \Xlink}{S \btok{}}.
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\ref{eq:boff} is a \emph{capacity} bound and nothing more: it says how
many riders' bytes fit, not what they cost. What they cost is two numbers, and
we measure both. A step pays a \textbf{fixed} cost $F$ for using the host path
at all, whatever the rider count, and a \textbf{per-request} cost $c$ --- the
part of a rider's transfer that does not hide behind the compute it is
prefetched against. On A100/PCIe at 2048 context, $F = 12.3$\,ms and
$c = 2.2$--$2.9$\,ms (\S\ref{sec:eval:cost}). \S\ref{sec:model:omits} charges
them both; neither belongs in Eq.~\ref{eq:boff}.

\subsection{(a) Context length cancels}
\label{sec:model:cancel}

The ratio of riders to residents,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cancel}
\frac{B_{\mathrm{off}}}{B_{\mathrm{cap}}}
  \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{ITL}\cdot \Xlink / (S \btok{})}{\Ckv / (S \btok{})}
  \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{ITL}\cdot \Xlink}{\Ckv},
\end{equation}
%
is independent of $S$. Long context does not make offloading more attractive:
it shrinks both cohorts equally. This is worth stating because the intuition
runs the other way --- long context is what makes KV cache expensive, so it
seems like the regime where moving KV off-device should pay most. It is not.
What a long context buys the rider in freed HBM, it spends again in per-step
transfer.

\paragraph{Validated on hardware, per model.} Equation~\ref{eq:step} claims that
ITL depends on batch and context only through their product, and that the
intercept is the weight term. On an A100-80 over 41 configurations both hold
\emph{within} each model --- $R^2 = 0.975$ for Llama-3.1-8B, $0.92$ for
Qwen2.5-32B --- and the intercept, which the equation predicts rather than fits,
comes out exactly for Qwen2.5-32B and 20\% high for Llama-3.1-8B. Pooling the
two models is meaningless and we do not report it: the intercept is
model-dependent, which is \S\ref{sec:model:modeldep}'s whole content.
Appendix~\ref{app:params} gives the fits, the residual structure, and why the
8B misses.

\subsection{(b) The step time is model-dependent}
\label{sec:model:modeldep}

At capacity a step reads all of device memory once, which invites the
simplification $\mathrm{ITL}_0 \approx \HBM/\BW$: a quantity fixed by the
device alone. It does not hold. Evaluating Eq.~\ref{eq:step} at each model's
own pool gives a full-pool ITL of $78.4$\,ms for Llama-3.1-8B against
$53.2$\,ms for Qwen2.5-32B --- a $1.47\times$ spread across two models on
identical hardware.
These two are \emph{projections}, not measurements: the largest working sets we
could fit on an 80\,GB part measure $69.7$ and $50.9$\,ms, and the full-pool
points extrapolate the $\effkv$ curve of \S\ref{sec:model:params} beyond them.

% src: paper/model/p4_qwen32b_a100.json @ 2bfc6ca5e
% NOTE: the full-pool step times are EXTRAPOLATED from these files via the
% eff_kv curve of appendix A, not read from them. Largest measured: 69.7 and
% 50.9 ms. The cause is that
weights and paged KV do not stream at the same efficiency
(\S\ref{sec:model:params}): a model that is mostly weights reads device memory
faster than one that is mostly KV, so $\mathrm{ITL}_0$ depends on the
weight-to-KV ratio and the applicability gate in (d) is model-dependent rather
than a property of the device.

The transfer budget is still $\mathrm{ITL}\cdot\Xlink$, and most of it is still
bought with \emph{weight-read time}. On GH200, Qwen-32B spends roughly
21\,ms of a 34\,ms step reading weights, during which nothing else contends for
the interconnect. The opportunity is time, not spare bandwidth. It is not free,
though: the arriving bytes are written into HBM, and those writes consume device
bandwidth during a step that is already HBM-bound.

\subsection{(c) The gain law}
\label{sec:model:gain}

Adding $B_{\mathrm{off}}$ riders to $B_{\mathrm{cap}}$ residents multiplies the
served batch by
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gain-exact}
\text{gain} \;=\; 1 + \frac{B_{\mathrm{off}}}{B_{\mathrm{cap}}}
      \;=\; 1 + \frac{\mathrm{ITL}_0 \cdot \Xlink}{\Ckv}.
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\ref{eq:gain-exact} is exact in the fluid limit and is the form to
compute with. Its structure is easier to see once you note that reading a full
pool takes roughly $\HBM$ divided by the bandwidth the device achieves over that
pool, so the gain is about $(\Xlink/\BW_{\mathrm{pool}})\cdot(\HBM/\Ckv)$. Two
things follow. The bandwidth ratio is a handicap --- $419$\,GB/s against a pool
that reads at $2000$--$2900$\,GB/s on a GH200, so $0.15$--$0.21$ --- and nothing
a serving system does improves it. The amplifier is $\HBM/\Ckv$: \textbf{the
value of offloading tracks how much of HBM the weights ate}, not model size and
not context length.

Equation~\ref{eq:gain-exact} is a \emph{capacity} result: it counts requests the
device can hold, not requests per second it serves. Two effects separate them
and \S\ref{sec:model:omits} charges both --- each rider displaces a share of a
resident's blocks for the window it streams into, which is why
Table~\ref{tab:gain}'s capacity column ($\times1.50$ for Qwen2.5-32B fp16) sits
below a hand recomputation of Eq.~\ref{eq:gain-exact} ($\times1.54$); and the
per-request cost lands on the step for every rider admitted. A third cost, the
fixed $F$, decides the paper.

\paragraph{Weight precision is the clean lever on $\HBM/\Ckv$.} Comparing models
of different sizes moves the weight footprint but also layer count, KV-head
count and architecture. The clean instrument is one model at two weight
precisions: the two Qwen2.5-32B rows share everything but the fact that fp8
weights leave $60.0$\,GB for KV where fp16 leave $26.5$. \textbf{Neither factor
of the multiplier is measured}, and we do not claim otherwise --- testing the
bandwidth ratio needs two interconnect generations and we have one, and testing
the amplifier needs that precision pair run end to end, which we have not done.
The rows are projections. It also has a practical edge
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}): quantizing \emph{weights} removes the headroom this
technique lives on, while quantizing \emph{KV} scales both cohorts together and
composes. Appendix~\ref{app:params} gives the sensitivity sweep and states what
is and is not held out.

\subsection{What the model omits, and the second gate}
\label{sec:model:omits}

Equation~\ref{eq:gain-exact} counts requests. A serving system is paid in requests
\emph{per second}, and the two differ by a term the gain law does not carry: a
fixed per-step cost $F$ for using the host path at all, independent of how many
riders use it. It is not small: we carry $F = 12.3$\,ms against a $54$\,ms step
on A100, and its uncertainty deserves stating because the paper's result turns
on it. We take it from the operator trace rather than from a fit: about ten
extra kernel launches per layer at $0.19$\,ms per layer
(\S\ref{sec:impl:cost}), so $12.2$\,ms on the 64-layer model it was measured on.
That derivation is what lets $F$ be charged per layer in
Table~\ref{tab:gain}, and it is the reason we prefer it to the rider sweep's
intercept, which two runs put at $9.6$ and $12.3$\,ms --- a $2.7$\,ms spread on
a three-point affine fit \S\ref{sec:eval:cost} rejects on other grounds. The two
routes agree at the top of that range, and we carry $12.3$\,ms as the
conservative reading. Being
launch-bound rather than bandwidth-bound, a faster link does not shrink it. Charging it gives the throughput form
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gain-tput}
\text{gain}_{\text{tput}} \;=\;
  \Big(1 + \tfrac{B_{\mathrm{off}}}{B_{\mathrm{cap}}}\Big)
  \cdot \frac{\mathrm{ITL}_0}{\mathrm{ITL}_0 + F + \Delta},
\end{equation}
%
where $\Delta = c\,B_{\mathrm{off}}$ is the per-request cost summed over the
admitted riders. This is a \textbf{second gate}, and it binds harder than the latency
gate of \S\ref{sec:model:gate}: the technique pays only when the capacity gain
exceeds the fixed-cost penalty, that is when
$B_{\mathrm{off}}/B_{\mathrm{cap}} > (F + \Delta)/\mathrm{ITL}_0$.

\textbf{Applied to Table~\ref{tab:gain}, this gate is what the paper turns on,
and two of four rows clear it.} Charging each row the $F$ it would pay, the
capacity gains of $\times$1.22 to $\times$1.67 become throughput ratios of
$\times$0.98, $\times$0.91, $\times$1.01 and $\times$1.05. The ordering is the
amplifier's: the two configurations where weights consume most of the device
pay, and the two where they do not, do not.

The margins are thin, and inverting Eq.~\ref{eq:gain-tput} is the useful way to
report them --- it gives the fixed cost each configuration can afford:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fstar}
F^{*} \;=\; \Big(\tfrac{B_{\mathrm{tot}}}{B_0} - 1\Big)\mathrm{ITL}_0 - \Delta.
\end{equation}
%
Eq.~\ref{eq:fstar} gives $F^{*} = 12.8$\,ms for Qwen2.5-32B fp16 against the
$12.3$ it pays, and $18.2$ against $15.4$ for Llama-70B fp8 --- margins of
$0.5$ and $2.8$\,ms. Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-32B fp8 miss, needing $23\%$ and
$44\%$ less $F$ than they pay.

\textbf{So the paper's positive claim is a margin of a few milliseconds in one
measured quantity.} That is why measuring $F$ on a coherent link is the first
experiment we ask of one, ahead of measuring its bandwidth: $F$ is launch-bound
rather than bandwidth-bound, so a faster interconnect does not touch it, but it
scales with the host CPU and the layer count, and a Grace host is not the A100
node's host. 

Equation~\ref{eq:gain-exact} also omits four smaller terms, listed so that a reader
can judge which might matter in a regime we did not measure. (One term
previously listed here is now charged in Table~\ref{tab:gain} instead: a rider's
KV crosses the link into its slot and is then read out of it, so it makes
\emph{two HBM passes per byte per step} --- its full $S\btok$, not the $k/L$ of
it resident at any instant, which is a factor of $L/k \approx 16$ larger than we
first wrote.) Host DRAM
bandwidth bounds $\Xlink$ from behind, and on the 480\,GB Grace configuration it
is of the same order as the link. And $B_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and $B_{\mathrm{off}}$
are counts of whole requests, so at long context both are small integers and the
fluid ratio of \S\ref{sec:model:cancel} is a poor approximation --- a point we
return to in \S\ref{sec:design:placement}. Finally, Eq.~\ref{eq:step} charges
every weight once, which is false for mixture-of-experts models; the law as
written does not apply to them.

\subsection{(d) The applicability gate}
\label{sec:model:gate}

Capacity binds only when the ITL SLO exceeds the full-pool ITL:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gate}
\mathrm{SLO} \;>\; \frac{W}{\BW\,\effw} + \frac{\Ckv}{\BW\,\effkv}.
\end{equation}
%
The gate is stated as a binary --- above the full-pool step time the technique
applies, below it the technique does not --- but its effect on the host cohort is
continuous. A target only slightly above $\mathrm{ITL}_0$ leaves room for very
few riders whatever the link delivers, which is why Table~\ref{tab:gain}'s rows
are SLO-bound rather than link-bound at 50\,ms.

Below the gate the scheduler is already refusing requests that HBM could hold,
because admitting them would break latency --- the system is latency-limited,
not capacity-limited, and offloading is then strictly harmful. Following (b),
this gate is model-dependent rather than a property of the device alone.

The gate scopes the technique to \emph{throughput-oriented} serving: batch
inference, long-form generation and reasoning workloads, where the latency
target is loose relative to a full-pool step. It excludes interactive chat. It
also excludes latency-sensitive agentic pipelines, which are often mis-filed as
throughput work: a tool-calling loop whose user is waiting has a tight target
even though its individual requests are long.

\subsection{Measured parameters}
\label{sec:model:params}

Both efficiencies in Eq.~\ref{eq:step} are measured on A100-80 across 41
single-shot configurations on two models. $\effw$, the weight-read efficiency,
is stable at $0.75$ of peak. $\effkv$, the paged-KV read efficiency, is
\textbf{not a constant}: it degrades by $1.6\times$ as the KV working set grows,
from $0.77$ at 4.3\,GB to $0.46$ at 51.5\,GB. That curve is measured on
Llama-3.1-8B alone --- Qwen2.5-32B's sweep reaches only a 6.4\,GB working set on an 80\,GB part,
well short of its 11.8\,GB pool, so
every point above it is 8B data --- and Table~\ref{tab:gain} applies it to
working sets of 19--76\,GB. The curve's last measured point is 51.5\,GB and the
interpolation clamps flat beyond it, so the Llama-8B row, which carries our
strongest negative prediction, sits 47\% past the data on a constant. It is worst exactly at the
capacity-bound operating point this paper is about --- so a roofline charging KV
at one fixed bandwidth is optimistic precisely where it matters. The
pool-weighted $\bar\eta$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:gain-exact} follows from the two, and runs
0.52--0.73 across Table~\ref{tab:gain}. Appendix~\ref{app:params} gives the
curve, the sensitivity of the projection to each parameter, and the evidence
that compute is not the binding term.

\subsection{A published result the model is consistent with}
\label{sec:model:directkv}

DirectKV~\cite{directkv} reports that its zero-copy host-KV attention is
slightly slower than an HBM-resident SGLang baseline on Llama-3.1-8B and wins on
OPT-13B and OPT-30B. Our amplifier offers an account of that ordering --- the 8B
leaves $\HBM/\Ckv \approx 1.26$, so there is little to buy, where OPT-30B's
60\,GB of weights triple it. We put it no more strongly than \emph{consistent
with}: Eq.~\ref{eq:gain-exact} compares a mixture against all-HBM while DirectKV is
all-host, their OPT wins are reported where the baseline runs out of memory and
OPT is multi-head attention with several times the KV bytes per token, and
OPT-13B has an amplifier close to the 8B's yet they win on it.
Appendix~\ref{app:directkv} gives the three objections in full. The contribution
does not rest on this.
