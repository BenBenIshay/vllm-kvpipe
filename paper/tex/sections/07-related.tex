\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Capacity, not compute, is what binds.} A trace-driven study of KV
offloading finds device memory capacity, not token budget, limits batch
size~\cite{bottlenecks}, and names scheduler improvements as future work. That is
the premise this paper builds on, measured rather than assumed.

\paragraph{The mechanism is solved; DirectKV is a competitor, not a substrate.}
DirectKV~\cite{directkv} contributes zero-copy GPU attention over CPU-resident KV
on coherent interconnects, eliminating the staging buffer. Its kernel reads host
memory \emph{during} the attention that needs it, where we prefetch layer
$\ell{+}1$ while layer $\ell$ computes: they pay no device memory and get no
overlap, we pay one $k$-window and hide about $90\%$ of the transfer. Neither
dominates on paper and settling it needs both on one machine, which needs their
SM90a-only kernel. Two things their paper establishes matter here. Its \S8.1
states the policy dimension is untouched --- the design ``does not change
scheduling, batching, or KV-management policies'' --- and its own analysis reaches
our PCIe conclusion from the kernel side, ``on PCIe, DirectKV should be viewed
primarily as a capacity-extension mechanism,'' while reporting NVLink-C2C cutting
attention latency up to $4.2\times$. Neither their evaluation nor ours covers a
70B-class configuration, which our model identifies as the most favourable point
of its curve, so that point is measured by nobody yet.
Appendix~\ref{app:related} gives the consequence for composition: a host-tier
request moved onto their kernel stops being a rider, because without a prefetch
window there is nothing left to hide.

\paragraph{The closest prior mechanism partitions by layer.} Pie~\cite{pie}
targets the same hardware, stages KV through GPU buffers as we do, prefetches per
layer so transfer overlaps compute, and reports up to $1.9\times$ over vLLM.
\textbf{The difference is the axis of partition.} Pie relocates $m$ of $n$
\emph{layers} of KV, ``using per-layer KV cache as the basic management unit'',
and that split applies to \emph{every request identically} under one global
expansion factor. So \textbf{no request's KV is ever fully absent from the
device} --- every request retains $(n-m)/n$ in HBM, always --- and
\textbf{interconnect bandwidth is spent uniformly}, a fixed fraction of every
request's KV rather than the whole KV of requests we select, which is what makes
\emph{which requests} a decision at all. The two axes are not orthogonal: they
spend the same link budget and in the fluid model reach the same interior point.
Our case for requests rests on what that model omits (\S\ref{sec:intro}), and one
thing favours layers, which we concede --- a layer split has no integer
quantization, so at long context it reaches operating points a request split
cannot express. Appendix~\ref{app:related} works both through and places NEO's
compute-offload alternative on the same axes.

\paragraph{Is decode serving memory-bound at all?} NanoFlow~\cite{nanoflow}
argues the opposite of \S\ref{sec:background}: that serving is
\emph{compute}-bound for common workloads once operations of different resource
classes are overlapped within a device, reporting $1.91\times$ over prior
engines. The boundary between the claims is exactly where this paper lives.
NanoFlow raises utilisation at a \emph{given} batch; our premise is that the
batch is capped by KV capacity before those units saturate. They compose in
principle, and collide if the arithmetic recovered by overlapping pushes the step
compute-bound, because then a host-tier request buys no throughput. At batch 142
for an 8B model at 4K --- far above the capacity limit on any device we consider
--- attention's arithmetic intensity has crossed the hardware balance point yet
the step is still memory-bound by more than $6\times$
(Appendix~\ref{app:params}). So the premises do not conflict at the batch sizes a
capacity limit permits; on a device where that margin closes, NanoFlow's premise
holds and ours does not.

\paragraph{The alternatives: move the request, or shrink the cache.}
Llumnix~\cite{llumnix} live-migrates requests \emph{across} instances --- the
natural alternative to a second tier, and the choice is economic rather than
technical, since migration needs a second instance with room while tiering needs
host DRAM that is already installed and idle. The two compose, a tier decision
being local and a migration decision global, and we do not evaluate the
combination. Architecturally, multi-head latent attention~\cite{deepseekv2}
compresses $K$ and $V$ into a low-rank latent, cutting $\btok$ by roughly an
order of magnitude, and sliding-window designs bound the cache by construction.
By Eq.~\ref{eq:cancel} a uniform reduction in $\btok$ scales both cohorts and
leaves their ratio unchanged, so shrinking KV does not by itself close the first
gate; what it does is raise $B_{\mathrm{cap}}$, amortising the fixed cost over
more requests while the capacity gain to be beaten is unchanged in ratio and
smaller in absolute requests. We regard a stack built entirely on MLA-class
models as outside this paper's regime rather than claim architecture-neutrality.

\paragraph{Summary of the gap.} Table~\ref{tab:axes} places the systems sharing
our hardware assumption on the two questions that matter: what unit of KV is
placed, and who decides. What is unsolved is which requests live in which tier,
how many host-tier requests a step can absorb without breaking the resident
cohort's latency, and when a request should move. DirectKV states it explicitly,
Fang et al.~\cite{calkv} close by calling for runtime policies, and
OmniServe~\cite{omniserve} takes its service classes as externally supplied
rather than inferring them.
