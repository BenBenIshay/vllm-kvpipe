\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We ask, in order: does a two-tier KV memory serve more requests than either
single-tier design; does the \emph{policy} contribute or would uniform
offloading do as well; what does a host-tier request cost and how does that
scale with the interconnect; does the capacity model of \S\ref{sec:model}
predict what we measure; and where does the technique stop paying.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Hardware.} A100-80 (PCIe~4) for the cost decomposition and the
capacity mechanism; GH200-96 (NVLink-C2C) for everything that depends on a
coherent interconnect. We \emph{measure} link bandwidth on both rather than
quoting it: the widely cited 900\,GB/s figure for C2C is bidirectional, and a
KV stream is unidirectional.

\paragraph{Models.} Qwen2.5-32B at fp16 and fp8 as the primary pair --- same
architecture, tokenizer and KV geometry, differing only in weight footprint, so
they move $\HBM/\Ckv$ and nothing else (\S\ref{sec:model}). Llama-3.1-8B as the
negative control. \needsgh{Llama-70B fp8 as a confirmatory point if time
allows.}

\paragraph{Workloads.} Three shapes, and the third is the one the placement
policy needs. \emph{Homogeneous}: equal contexts and generation lengths,
arriving together --- the simplest capacity test, and what our A100 results
below use. \emph{Poisson arrivals} at varying rate, for SLO attainment.
\emph{Heterogeneous}: mixed remaining-generation lengths and multi-turn idle
gaps.

The third is a requirement rather than a refinement, and the reason is worth
stating because it is easy to get wrong. Our placement rule scores requests on
\emph{temporal} signals --- idle time and estimated remaining generation
(\S\ref{sec:design:placement}) --- because context length provably cannot
discriminate. A workload in which every request has the same generation length
and never idles therefore gives every request the same score, and scored
placement degenerates to arrival order \emph{by construction}. Measuring the
two as equal on such a workload would say nothing about the policy; it would
only say the workload had nothing to choose between.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Stock vLLM (all device-tier); all-host, i.e.\ every
request offloaded, which is the placement DirectKV makes by construction;
uniform offload at a matched byte budget, which is the placement both Pie and
DirectKV instantiate at their own granularities; and our policy.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Requests served at a fixed ITL SLO first, tokens/s second,
p99 ITL, and achieved interconnect bandwidth so that ``the transfer hides'' is
measured rather than inferred. \textbf{We do not report mean concurrency.} It
averages over sampled steps, so an arm that serves requests more slowly holds
them longer and scores \emph{higher}; it is not a goodput metric, and we
retracted a result that relied on it.

\subsection{Is the policy load-bearing?}
\label{sec:eval:ablation}

If uniform offloading at the same interconnect budget does as well, the policy
layer is decoration and the contribution collapses to the analysis. This is the
experiment that decides it.

\needsgh{plan §4.3. Arms add one mechanism each: uniform at a matched byte
budget; our admission deciding how many while arrival order decides which;
$+$ scored placement; $+$ migration. Run on the heterogeneous workload of
\S\ref{sec:eval:setup} --- on a homogeneous one the scored arm cannot differ
from arrival. Report tok/s and, for every pair, the bytes each arm moved.}

\textbf{Matched budget, not matched request count.} Capping how many requests
are host-tier does not control this comparison: the policies select requests of
different contexts and therefore move different volume, so part of any delta
would be a difference in offload ratio wearing the costume of a policy win. We
count bytes in both directions per arm and treat a pair differing by more than
$1.25\times$ in traffic as not comparable.

\needsgh{The adaptive probe's verdict. On PCIe it measured demotion as
\emph{harmful} and correctly reverted; here it should measure \emph{beneficial}
and keep it. If it still says harmful, the host path has a fixed per-step cost
that survives a fast link --- a finding either way, and one we would rather
publish than omit.}

\subsection{What a host-tier request costs}
\label{sec:eval:cost}

Sweeping the number of host-tier requests on an otherwise identical engine and
fitting $\mathrm{ITL}(r) = \mathrm{ITL}_0 + \text{fixed} + r\cdot c$ separates
a fixed per-step cost of using the host path at all from a per-request cost
that fails to overlap. The two extrapolate to completely different answers on a
faster link, which is why a single operating point cannot decide the question.

\label{sec:eval:negative}

\subsection{Correctness}
\label{sec:eval:correct}

Placement moves bytes rather than approximating them, so the gates below are
hygiene rather than a contribution. Numerically we verify the host pool's round
trip, the two-tier attention against a single-pass reference over the same KV
(576 mixed decode layer-steps, $\max|\mathrm{diff}| = 0$), and the prefill
handover across block boundaries and after the producer's memory has been
recycled. End to end we compare generated tokens against stock vLLM.

One methodological note, because it traps anyone reproducing this.
\texttt{bf16} attention reductions are not associative, so their result depends
on batch composition: stock vLLM produces different text for the same prompt at
concurrency 1 and at 6, reproducibly at each. Any system that changes batching
--- as a tier does --- will diverge on such prompts without being wrong. We
therefore compare serially, batch of one on both sides, and under load only on
prompts pre-screened as batching-stable. For calibration: on a serial greedy
comparison our host tier matched stock vLLM on 6 of 6 prompts in one round and
4 of 6 in another, while enabling \emph{vLLM's own prefix cache} --- changing
nothing else --- matched on 2 of 6.
